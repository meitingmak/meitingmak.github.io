## Overview

Hot Jupiters are highly irradiated gas giants that typically exhibit strong day–night temperature contrasts and complex atmospheric circulation patterns. 
Photochemical hazes are expected to form in their atmospheres through ultraviolet-driven chemical reactions and can subsequently be transported by atmospheric winds, removed through gravitational settling, and interact with incoming stellar radiation.
My research investigates how atmospheric dynamics, haze transport, and radiative feedback influence the 3D spatial distribution of photochemical hazes on hot Jupiters, and how these processes affect their observable atmospheric signatures.

Using the 3D General Circulation Model (GCM), the Unified Model (UM), alongside analytical approaches, I aim to connect the underlying atmospheric physics with observations from facilities such as the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST).

## The Impact of Different Haze Types on Hot-Jupiter Atmospheres

In Mak et al. (2025), I investigated the atmospheric effects of three different haze types, namely Titan-like, water-world-like, and soot-like hazes, on three hot Jupiters: HD 189733b, HD 209458b, and WASP-39b.
Using the UM, we simulated the transport, gravitational settling, and radiative effects of photochemical hazes with different optical properties.
Our results demonstrated that haze radiative feedback can significantly modify atmospheric temperature structures and circulation. In particular, more strongly absorbing hazes can strengthen the equatorial superrotating jet and alter the spatial distribution of haze.
We also identified the distinct roles of atmospheric circulation in controlling haze transport. While the superrotating jet largely governs the day–night distribution, atmospheric eddies influence the latitudinal distribution, and divergent and eddy winds contribute to the finer structure of haze distributions.
Importantly, our simulations suggested that photochemical hazes can produce greater opacity at the morning terminator than at the evening terminator, potentially leading to wavelength-dependent differences in transit depth.
These findings suggest that morning–evening limb asymmetry, particularly at ultraviolet and optical wavelengths, could provide an observational diagnostic of photochemical hazes.

## Understanding Flow-driven Limb Asymmetry

Building upon the results of Mak et al. (2025), I developed an analytical framework in Mak et al. (2026) to investigate how atmospheric transport and gravitational settling determine the distribution of haze particles between the morning and evening limbs of hot Jupiters.

The framework considers the competition between atmospheric advection and gravitational settling, incorporating planetary properties such as gravity, radius, and stellar irradiation.

By comparing analytical predictions with three-dimensional climate simulations, we demonstrated that the maximum particle size capable of reaching the morning limb depends strongly on planetary gravity and atmospheric circulation.

For low-gravity hot Jupiters, relatively large particles can remain suspended and be transported towards the morning limb. In contrast, stronger gravitational settling on high-gravity planets limits the transport of larger particles.

This framework provides a computationally efficient approach for estimating haze particle-size asymmetries and identifying promising targets for limb-resolved observations.

## Implications for Observations

Understanding the interplay between atmospheric dynamics, haze transport, and radiative feedback is essential for interpreting the transmission spectra of hot Jupiters.

Our results suggest that photochemical hazes can produce wavelength-dependent differences between the morning and evening terminators, particularly in the ultraviolet and optical wavelength regimes.

Such asymmetries may provide an additional observational diagnostic for distinguishing photochemical hazes from other aerosol populations and constraining their physical properties.

By combining three-dimensional simulations with analytical approaches, my research aims to guide observing strategies and improve the interpretation of atmospheric observations from JWST, Ariel, and future facilities.
