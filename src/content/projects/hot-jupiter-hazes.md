## Overview

Hot Jupiters are highly irradiated gas giants that orbit close to their host star. They typically exhibit strong day–night temperature contrasts and complex atmospheric circulation patterns. Photochemical hazes are expected to form in their atmospheres through UV-driven chemical reactions (i.e., photochemistry) and can subsequently be transported by atmospheric winds, removed through gravitational settling, and interact with incoming stellar radiation.

<div style="height: 20px;"></div>

My research investigates how atmospheric dynamics, haze transport, and its radiative feedback influence the 3D spatial distribution of photochemical hazes on hot Jupiters, and how these processes affect their observable features detected by telescopes. Using the 3D General Circulation Model (GCM), the Unified Model (UM), alongside analytical approaches, I aim to connect the underlying atmospheric physics with observations from facilities such as the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST).

<div style="height: 80px;"></div>

## The Impact of Different Haze Types on Hot-Jupiter Atmospheres

In Mak et al. (2025), I investigated the radiative impact of three different haze types with different optical properties, namely Titan-like, water-world-like, and soot-like hazes, on three hot Jupiters: HD 189733b, HD 209458b, and WASP-39b. Using the UM, we demonstrated that haze radiative feedback can significantly modify atmospheric temperature structures and circulation. 

<div style="height: 20px;"></div>


We also identified the distinct roles of atmospheric circulation in controlling haze transport. While the super-rotating jet largely governs the day–night haze distribution, atmospheric eddies influence the latitudinal haze distribution, and divergent and eddy winds contribute to the finer structure of haze distributions. Importantly, our simulations suggested that photochemical hazes can be trapped around the nightside gyres around the morning limb, therefore producing greater opacity at the morning limb than at the evening limb. This can lead to wavelength-dependent differences in the limb transit depth. These findings suggest that morning–evening limb asymmetry, particularly at UV and optical wavelengths, could provide an observational diagnostic of photochemical hazes.

Building upon the results of Mak et al. (2025), I developed an analytical framework in Mak et al. (2026) to investigate how atmospheric transport and gravitational settling determine the distribution of haze particles between the morning and evening limbs of hot Jupiters.

<div style="height: 20px;"></div>

The framework considers the competition between atmospheric advection and gravitational settling, incorporating planetary properties such as gravity, particle radius, and stellar irradiation. By comparing analytical predictions with 3D climate simulations, we demonstrated that the maximum particle size capable of reaching the morning limb depends strongly on planetary gravity and atmospheric circulation. For low-gravity hot Jupiters, relatively large particles can remain suspended and be transported towards the morning limb. In contrast, stronger gravitational settling on high-gravity planets limits the transport of larger particles. This framework provides a computationally efficient approach for estimating haze particle-size asymmetries and identifying promising targets for limb-resolved observations.
